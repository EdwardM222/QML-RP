\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-background}

Near-term \ac{qml} is largely centred on hybrid variational models, in which \glspl{parameterised-quantum-circuit} are trained through classical optimisation~\cite{du_quantum_2025}. \acp{vqc} apply this approach to classification by encoding input data, applying trainable circuit operations and measuring the resulting class probabilities. Their behaviour is strongly dependent on architecture, with closely related circuits able to produce substantially different predictive and optimisation results~\cite{pinto_composing_2026,cerezo_challenges_2022}.

The principal contribution of this dissertation is \texttt{QuantumECOC}, a custom quantum multiclass \gls{ensemble} framework implemented through \glslink{ecoc-decomposition}{\ac{ecoc} decomposition}. This reduces the task learned by each base circuit from multiclass classification to a \gls{binary-class-split}, allowing the ensemble to be constructed from small and computationally restricted \acp{vqc}. This shifts the principal opportunity for improving each learner towards the design of its circuit and encoded information rather than increasing its output complexity. The changing class partition assigned to each learner also introduces task diversity because every circuit models a different separation of the original classes. \texttt{QuantumECOC} manages these partitions, constructs the learners, assigns their features and samples, and combines their evidence into a multiclass prediction through one configurable and traceable implementation.

Ensemble learning can reduce dependence on one selected circuit, but its performance remains dependent on the information and decision capacity available to its learners~\cite{dong_survey_2020,incudini_resource_2023,radojicic_reducing_2026}. The main empirical analysis therefore examines the design space of this framework at two connected levels. A configurable \gls{ansatz} and \ac{vqc} implementation allows the characteristics of the base circuits to be investigated systematically. The retained circuits are then evaluated as components of varying \texttt{QuantumECOC} ensemble architectures.

\Gls{validation-weighted-decoding} of the \ac{ecoc} outputs provides the direct operating mode of \texttt{QuantumECOC}, with classical and measured quantum \gls{stacking} implemented as supporting aggregation comparisons. The principal innovation is \texttt{CoherentECOC}, which implements \gls{coherent-aggregation} by retaining the pretrained base circuits as unmeasured components of a joint quantum circuit. It changes the \gls{measurement-boundary} as well as the combiner, allowing a trainable meta-circuit to operate on the joint base-register state before the final readout rather than learning from measured classical outputs.

\section{Problem and Research Gap}
\label{sec:intro-problem-gap}

Previous research shows that \ac{vqc} performance is sensitive to circuit design and that ensembles of restricted quantum classifiers can offer an alternative to a single larger model~\cite{friedrich_quantum_2025,incudini_resource_2023,pinto_composing_2026}. These questions are generally investigated separately, however. Architecture studies concentrate on individual circuits, while ensemble studies frequently begin with a predetermined learner and aggregation rule. Consequently, there is limited controlled evidence connecting the design of a configurable quantum component to its subsequent behaviour inside a multiclass ensemble.

% This separation also obscures the methodological work required to move from a binary \ac{vqc} to a complete multiclass ensemble. A common framework must generate the class decomposition, construct and train the base circuits, allocate features and samples, decode their outputs and retain enough configuration and resource information for later comparison. Without this common implementation, apparent differences between ensemble methods may arise from changes in preprocessing, class partitioning, learner composition or feature allocation rather than the aggregation mechanism itself.

Comparisons between aggregation methods can also be difficult to interpret when the learner pool, feature assignments or ensemble size change at the same time. An apparent improvement may arise from the revised ensemble rather than the method used to combine its outputs. Direct and stacked aggregation must therefore be compared using the same underlying learners and class decomposition.

Measured quantum stacking operates after each \gls{base-learner} has produced a classical output. Replacing the classical combiner with a quantum model does not remove this boundary because the measured values must first be encoded into a new circuit. Existing coherent proposals demonstrate that classifier contributions can instead be combined before measurement, but their construction differs from an ensemble of independently trained \acp{vqc}~\cite{schuld_quantum_2018,tolotti_weighted_2025}. It remains unclear whether a trainable coherent model built from such components can provide benefits sufficient to justify its additional cost.

This dissertation addresses the gap through an end-to-end configurable implementation centred on \texttt{QuantumECOC}. Its principal empirical analysis follows the custom \ac{vqc} components into direct ensembles and determines how their design and composition affect the resulting system. Classical and measured quantum stacking provide controlled aggregation references, while the principal innovation, \texttt{CoherentECOC}, tests whether the same pretrained components can instead be combined before measurement. This structure separates the behaviour of the underlying ensemble from the effect of the aggregation mechanism.

\section{Aim, Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro-aim-rqs}

\subsection{Overall Aim}

The aim of this dissertation is to design and evaluate \texttt{QuantumECOC} as a configurable quantum multiclass ensemble framework, analyse how its \ac{vqc} components and ensemble design affect predictive performance, stability and computational requirements, and extend the framework through the measurement-free \texttt{CoherentECOC} aggregation method.

\subsection{Research Objectives}

The research objectives are to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Implement a configurable ansatz and \ac{vqc} component that supports consistent circuit construction, training, prediction, resetting and resource tracing.
    \item Implement \texttt{QuantumECOC} as a deterministic multiclass ensemble framework that manages \ac{ecoc} decomposition, learner construction, feature and sample allocation, training, direct decoding and result recording.
    \item Conduct a cross-dataset \gls{architecture-search} analysis of how \ac{vqc} structure affects the performance, stability, information capacity and computational requirements of the available base components.
    \item Extend this analysis to determine how learner count, architecture composition, feature availability and \gls{prediction-diversity} affect the complete \texttt{QuantumECOC} ensemble.
    \item Implement classical and measured quantum stacking as matched aggregation comparisons against validation-weighted direct decoding.
    \item Introduce and evaluate \texttt{CoherentECOC}, a measurement-free extension that combines pretrained \acp{vqc} through a trainable meta-circuit before final measurement.
    % \item assess whether the principal findings remain credible under device-derived noise and restricted hardware execution where feasible.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions}

The dissertation addresses the following research questions:

    \item How are direct \texttt{QuantumECOC} results affected by learner count, base-architecture composition, information availability and prediction diversity?

\section{Contributions and Scope}

\subsection{Contributions}

The principal contribution of the dissertation is the design and implementation of \texttt{QuantumECOC} as a configurable quantum multiclass ensemble framework. Its principal innovation is \texttt{CoherentECOC}, which extends the framework so that pretrained base learners can be combined through a trainable quantum circuit without intermediate measurement. These contributions are supported by a large empirical analysis of the \ac{vqc} and ensemble design choices that determine their behaviour.

The complete contribution therefore comprises:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The purpose-built \texttt{QuantumECOC} class, which manages deterministic multiclass decomposition, custom \ac{vqc} construction, learner-specific data allocation, training, validation-weighted decoding and result tracing.
    \item The \texttt{CoherentECOC} innovation, which transfers pretrained base ansätze and parameters into a joint circuit and applies a trainable meta-circuit before the only final measurement.

\subsection{Scope and Delimitations}

The empirical study focuses on supervised multiclass classification using small and moderate tabular datasets. Two-qubit \acp{vqc} are used as the principal ensemble learners so that the binary \ac{ecoc} tasks remain computationally restricted and several learners can be combined. \ac{ecoc} serves as practical multiclass decomposition scaffolding rather than a contribution to coding theory. The Stage III findings therefore characterise ensembles of small binary learners and should not be assumed to remain unchanged when the base-circuit width or task formulation is increased.

The broad model searches and coherent experiments are conducted through ideal \gls{statevector-simulation} because differentiable quantum simulation increases rapidly with circuit width and ensemble size. The dissertation evaluates observed performance--resource trade-offs and implementation feasibility, it does not attempt to establish a general quantum speed-up or universal superiority over classical learning.

\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}

This chapter reviews the ideas needed to position the dissertation within classical ensemble learning and near-term \ac{qml}. The discussion first introduces classical ensemble structure design, covering diversity, stacking and multiclass decomposition. It then examines the design and trainability of \acp{vqc}, before comparing quantum ensembles and various stacking approaches. \section{Classical Ensemble Foundations}
\label{sec:rw-classical-ensembles}

\subsection{Diversity, Bagging and Learned Aggregation}
Dong et al.~\cite{dong_survey_2020} describe ensemble learning as the combination of several predictive models to obtain a result that is more accurate or stable than the constituent learners considered separately. Common approaches include \gls{bagging}, \gls{boosting}, random forests, random-subspace methods and stacking. Their effectiveness does not follow automatically from increasing the number of learners. An ensemble benefits when its members are individually useful while making sufficiently different errors, creating a balance between predictive strength, diversity, stability and computational complexity.

Classical random-subspace methods create this variation by exposing learners to different feature subsets rather than requiring every model to process the complete input. Radojicic et al.~\cite{radojicic_reducing_2026} similarly use feature reduction to decrease the resources required by quantum ensembles while retaining variation between their learners. Architectural diversity alone is not sufficient, however, Pinto et al.~\cite{pinto_composing_2026} report that disagreement between base learner predictions can be more informative than either the individual \glslink{macro-f1}{\ac{f1}} or the structural difference between circuits. Diversity-aware approaches such as boosting therefore treat disagreement as an explicit optimisation variable alongside prediction loss and regularisation~\cite{liu_construction_2025}. These findings motivate varying both the circuit structures and feature subsets of the base learners, while examining their realised prediction diversity rather than assuming that different architectures necessarily produce complementary errors.

\subsection{Out-of-Fold Stacking and Model Selection}
Stacking replaces a fixed prediction aggregation rule with a learned \gls{meta-learner}. Following the modified stacking approach examined by Demir and Dalkilic~\cite{demir_modified_2018}, the predictions of the base learners form a second-level feature representation from which a combiner model learns the final decision. This permits interactions between learner outputs to influence the result, rather than assigning every learner a fixed or independently calculated contribution.

The meta-training representation must be constructed from predictions for samples that were not used to fit the corresponding base learners. \glslink{out-of-fold}{\ac{oof} construction} satisfies this requirement by training the base models on a subset of the training partition and predicting the held-out fold. Repeating the process across all folds produces meta-features without exposing the combiner to in-sample base predictions. Hybrid ensemble research has used the same principle to construct learned features before downstream quantum classification~\cite{xiong_hybrid_2024}. Demir and Dalkilic also show that feature-subspace generation, validation-driven learner selection and the choice of combiner can alter class-level outcomes~\cite{demir_modified_2018}. Stacking must therefore be treated as a separate model design rather than a neutral replacement for direct aggregation, and its additional training cost must be considered alongside any predictive improvement.

\subsection{\acs*{ecoc}}

\ac{ecoc} provide a method of decomposing a multiclass classification problem into a series of binary predictions. Following the formulation described by Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen_optimal_2021}, each base learner divides the target classes into two groups and learns to distinguish between them. The grouping changes between learners, allowing each one to examine a different separation of the original classes.

The binary predictions are then combined to identify the target class whose expected pattern most closely matches the outputs of the learners. Since the final decision is based on several different class groupings, an individual incorrect prediction does not necessarily produce an incorrect multiclass result.

This decomposition is particularly useful for restricted quantum learners. Each circuit only needs to represent a binary decision boundary, reducing the output complexity required from an individual \ac{vqc} and allowing circuit architecture, feature encoding and trainable capacity to become the principal learner-level design variables. The distinct class groupings also create diversity at the task level, even when the learners use identical ansätze, because their binary targets represent different views of the original multiclass problem.

\section{Variational Quantum Classification}
\label{sec:rw-vqc}

\subsection{Near-Term \acs*{vqc} Workflow}
Early \ac{qml} research considered both the acceleration of expensive classical learning subroutines and the reformulation of probabilistic learning through quantum information processing~\cite{schuld_introduction_2015}. Large theoretical speed-ups are generally associated with fault-tolerant algorithms and efficient access to quantum data, whereas current hardware is more commonly studied through hybrid variational models~\cite{du_quantum_2025}. \acp{vqc} belong to this second group and use a parameterised quantum circuit as a trainable classifier within a classical optimisation loop.

The standard workflow described by Du et al.~\cite{du_quantum_2025} consists of encoding classical features into a quantum state, applying parameterised transformations, measuring selected circuit outputs, calculating a loss and updating the parameters classically. The quantum circuit determines the accessible feature transformation, while the optimiser and loss provide the training procedure. Resch and Karpuzcu~\cite{resch_quantum_2019} emphasise that measurement exposes only limited information about a quantum state, so model outputs must be defined through probabilities or expectation values rather than direct access to the underlying amplitudes.

The practical value of a \ac{vqc} cannot therefore be inferred only from its nominal qubit count or Hilbert-space dimension. Biamonte et al.~\cite{biamonte_quantum_2017} identify data loading, state preparation and output reconstruction as important parts of the total computational cost, while Du et al.~\cite{du_quantum_2025} distinguish circuit expressivity from trainability and generalisation. These limitations make controlled classical baselines, repeated optimisation runs and explicit resource reporting necessary when evaluating near-term quantum classifiers.

\subsection{Encoding, Re-uploading and Ansatz Design}
\Gls{data-encoding} maps classical features through parameterised gates before they can influence a \ac{vqc}. Angle encoding associates individual feature values with rotation angles, while interaction-based encodings can introduce products or relationships between features. A single encoding layer restricts the frequencies available to the model. \Gls{data-reuploading} addresses this restriction by interleaving repeated feature encodings with trainable operations, increasing the accessible function class without necessarily increasing the \gls{circuit-width}~\cite{du_quantum_2025}. McFarthing et al.~\cite{mcfarthing_classical_2024} relate repeated single-qubit feature rotations to a larger Fourier spectrum, although the additional \gls{circuit-depth} also increases the exposure to noise and optimisation difficulty.

The ansatz refers to how these encodings are arranged with trainable rotations and entangling gates. Its behaviour depends not only on which gates are present but on their order, distribution and connectivity. Salmenpera et al.~\cite{salmenpera_impact_2024} show that feature-map placement can materially change the resulting quantum model and can leave apparently present circuit operations functionally redundant. Entanglement can provide interactions across qubits, but broader connectivity and additional trainable entanglers increase both the parameter count and the two-qubit gate cost.

The number of interacting design choices creates what is often described as the ansatz lottery. Qubit count, encoding style, feature allocation, re-uploading, trainable-layer placement, entangling gate and connectivity can each take several forms, and these possibilities compound into a very large architecture space. Consequently, circuits with similar widths and depths can still differ substantially in their trainability and predictive performance. Pinto et al.~\cite{pinto_composing_2026} observe this variation between related \ac{vqc} structures, while hardware-oriented research favours shallow templates and restricted entanglement to reduce optimisation and noise costs~\cite{friedrich_quantum_2025,park_addressing_2025}. There is therefore no single architecture that can be assumed to perform well across all datasets, supporting the empirical evaluation of these variables rather than relying on one fixed ansatz.

\subsection{Trainability and Scaling}
\ac{vqc} optimisation is sensitive to the initial parameter distribution, circuit depth, data encoding and selected cost function~\cite{vasan_exploring_2025,friedrich_quantum_2025}. A central trainability problem is the \gls{barren-plateau}, in which gradients become too small to provide a useful optimisation direction. Cerezo et al.~\cite{cerezo_challenges_2022} relate this behaviour to circuit expressivity, global cost functions and increasing system size. The risk is therefore not resolved by making a circuit uniformly wider or deeper; the added representational capacity may become inaccessible to the optimiser.

Structured initialisation, local cost functions and restricted circuit designs are common responses~\cite{du_quantum_2025}. Friedrich and Maziero~\cite{friedrich_quantum_2025} further demonstrate that replacing one larger \ac{qnn} with an ensemble of shallower components can increase gradient variance and reduce parameterisation complexity. Their findings connect circuit scale to the later ensemble question: distributing a task across trainable components may be preferable to adding all available capacity to one circuit.

\section{Quantum Ensemble and Stacking Methods}
\label{sec:rw-quantum-ensembles}

\subsection{Measured Quantum Ensembles}
\Gls{measured-aggregation} executes component learners separately and combines the resulting classical outputs. Incudini et al.~\cite{incudini_resource_2023} apply bagging and AdaBoost to \acp{qnn} using different data-loading configurations, reporting reductions in qubit, parameter and gate requirements together with evaluations under noiseless simulation, backend-derived noise and real superconducting hardware. Friedrich and Maziero~\cite{friedrich_quantum_2025} approach modularity from the trainability perspective, while Park et al.~\cite{park_addressing_2025} partition high-dimensional inputs across a multi-chip \ac{vqc} ensemble. Although their implementations differ, each evaluates whether restricted quantum components can collectively replace a more demanding monolithic model.

Other work aims to adapt specific classical ensemble mechanisms, McFarthing et al.~\cite{mcfarthing_classical_2024} construct bagging, boosting and logistic-regression ensembles from single-qubit variational classifiers. Song et al.~\cite{song_quantum_2024} use quantum prediction probabilities as confidence information within AdaBoost.Q, experimentally evaluating the method on a superconducting processor. Bagging with a \ac{qsvc} has also produced application-level improvements in heart-disease classification~\cite{abdulsalam_explainable_2023}. Although these results show that measured quantum learners can benefit from classical combination, their different datasets, circuit models and evaluation conditions prevent the reported gains from establishing a general ensemble advantage.

Some proposals move more of the ensemble construction into the quantum procedure. Rathi and Kumar~\cite{rathi_enhanced_2025} use controlled operations to create transformed feature--label trajectories in superposition before applying a shared shallow classifier, while their unsupervised quantum bagging method uses QMeans learners and quantum sampling to improve resistance to corrupted labels~\cite{rathi_quantum_2025}. Such methods expand the available design space but depend on specific state-preparation or memory-access assumptions and do not correspond directly to an ensemble of independently trained \acp{vqc}. Across these approaches, the predominant practical pattern remains the same.

\subsection{Measured and Hybrid Quantum Stacking}
Stacking extends measured ensembles by learning how the base outputs should be combined. Vasan et al.~\cite{vasan_exploring_2025} combine multiple quantum classifier families in a stacking framework for industrial-control security, while Kang and Shin~\cite{kang_amino_2025} integrate machine-learning, deep-learning and \ac{qnn} outputs through a learned meta-model. These studies indicate that quantum predictions can contribute complementary information even when an individual \ac{qnn} is not the strongest constituent model.

The location of the quantum component varies across hybrid stackers. Xiong et al.~\cite{xiong_hybrid_2024} use \ac{oof} classical stacking predictions as a reduced feature representation before downstream quantum classification. In the \ac{seqsvm} approach, Akrom et al.~\cite{akrom_bloodbrain_2025} combine the outputs of classical boosting models and encode the resulting meta-features for a quantum support vector machine. Conversely, measured \ac{vqc} or \ac{qnn} outputs may be passed to a classical combiner~\cite{mcfarthing_classical_2024,vasan_exploring_2025}. These arrangements evaluate whether a classical or quantum model can learn relationships between predictions.

\subsection{Coherent and State-Preserving Aggregation}
State-preserving ensembles attempt to perform the aggregation before the base information is converted into independent classical predictions. Schuld and Petruccione~\cite{schuld_quantum_2018} propose representing multiple quantum classifiers through superposition, while Araujo and da Silva~\cite{araujo_quantum_2020} examine an ensemble in which the contributions of trained classifiers are encoded and weighted within a quantum procedure. These approaches use quantum parallelism at the ensemble level, but require specialised representations of the classifier set rather than the simple concatenation of separately trained variational circuits.

Amplitude-based aggregation provides a related theoretical direction. Safina~\cite{safina_quantum_2023} applies quantum amplitude amplification to estimate the total class probability produced by a random-forest ensemble, with a theoretical quadratic reduction in the number of classifier queries. However, the simulated procedure loads classically calculated tree probabilities into quantum amplitudes rather than executing the trained base classifiers on quantum circuits.

Tolotti et al.~\cite{tolotti_weighted_2025} move closer to a coherent ensemble by using control registers to index homogeneous quantum classifiers. Validation measurements are used to estimate learner quality, after which the learned weights are encoded for coherent test-time combination. The method demonstrates that internal classifier contributions can be weighted within one quantum circuit, while also showing sensitivity to the accuracy of finite-shot weight estimates. More broadly, indexed data access, amplitude preparation, controlled classifier execution and post-selection can impose costs that are not reflected by the logical classifier count alone~\cite{schuld_quantum_2018,tolotti_weighted_2025}.

\section{Synthesis and Dissertation Gap}
\label{sec:rw-gap}

The literature establishes that \ac{vqc} performance depends on circuit design and that ensembles can distribute a learning problem across smaller quantum components. Feature allocation, learner diversity and the aggregation rule may all influence the collective result~\cite{incudini_resource_2023,mcfarthing_classical_2024,park_addressing_2025}. Structural difference alone does not guarantee useful diversity, making the observed predictions of the learners as important as their nominal architectures~\cite{pinto_composing_2026,radojicic_reducing_2026}.

Existing studies examine separate parts of the resulting design space. These include quantum bagging and boosting, hybrid meta-learning, amplitude-based probability estimation and coherent classifier weighting~\cite{song_quantum_2024,akrom_bloodbrain_2025,safina_quantum_2023,tolotti_weighted_2025}. Their classifier families, data assignments and state-access assumptions differ, preventing their reported results from providing a controlled comparison of aggregation methods.

None of the reviewed approaches provides an end-to-end configurable implementation that constructs a deterministic ensemble of \acp{vqc}, systematically analyses its design space, and then retains the same ensemble across different aggregation methods. The dissertation aims to address this gap through the \texttt{QuantumECOC} framework and its \texttt{CoherentECOC} innovation, supported by controlled cross-dataset analysis and matched aggregation comparisons.

\chapter{Approach and Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

\section{Research Design and Experimental Progression}
\label{sec:method-research-design}

The research was structured around the implementation and analysis of \texttt{QuantumECOC}. The analysis was structured as a staged empirical investigation in which model complexity increased while the number of candidate configurations was progressively reduced, allowing the main \ac{vqc} and ensemble analysis to inform the more expensive aggregation experiments. This design was influenced by previous model-selection approaches that generated a large initial candidate pool before retaining a smaller set for later ensemble experiments~\cite{demir_modified_2018}. It also responded to unresolved relationships between ensemble size, model diversity, predictive performance and training cost~\cite{dong_survey_2020,pinto_composing_2026}.

Stage I established classical reference performance. Stages II and III formed the main empirical analysis: Stage II examined the configurable \ac{vqc} components and Stage III determined how those components behaved inside the complete \texttt{QuantumECOC} framework. Stage IV implemented classical and measured quantum stacking as supporting aggregation comparisons. Stage V introduced the principal innovation, \texttt{CoherentECOC}, which reconstructed pretrained base learners within a joint circuit so that aggregation occurred before final measurement.

Each stage inherited the fixed datasets, interfaces and viable designs established previously, introducing only the variables required for the next experimental question. Candidate advancement followed the criteria in Section~\ref{sec:method-progressive-selection}. The Stacked and Coherent comparisons retained the same \ac{ecoc} matrix, learner count, base architectures and feature assignments. This progression separated changes caused by the aggregation method from those caused by rebuilding or reconfiguring the underlying ensemble.

\section{Implemented Framework Architecture}
\label{sec:method-framework-architecture}

\texttt{QuantumECOC} formed the centre of the implementation. Given an ensemble configuration, it generated the multiclass decomposition, constructed and trained the required binary learners, allocated learner-specific data, decoded their outputs and retained their predictive and resource information. The supporting ansatz layer represented circuits as configurable sequences of encoding, trainable and entangling operations, while the \texttt{VQC} class converted these structures into reusable learners with a common interface for construction, training, probability prediction, resetting and tracing.

Validation-weighted \ac{ecoc} decoding formed the direct operating mode of \texttt{QuantumECOC}. \texttt{StackedECOC} reused the same ensemble state but exposed the measured learner probabilities to either a classical or quantum meta-learner. \texttt{CoherentECOC} reused the learned ansätze and parameters while changing the interface between the base and meta levels: the learners were placed on disjoint registers, their intermediate measurements were removed and a trainable meta-circuit was appended before the final readout. The four evaluated aggregation conditions therefore arose from a common ensemble definition rather than independent implementations.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:method-setup}

\subsection{Implementation Framework}

The experimental framework combined PennyLane and PyTorch to construct and train the quantum models. PennyLane was used to define the parameterised quantum circuits as differentiable \glspl{qnode}, which were integrated with PyTorch through \texttt{qml.qnn.TorchLayer}. This provided a common training procedure for the individual \acp{vqc}, \ac{ecoc} ensembles and quantum stacking models. Scikit-learn was used for dataset preprocessing, data splitting, classical reference models, classical meta-learners and evaluation metrics.

The implementation was divided across three main components. \texttt{Ansatze.py} defined the reusable circuit layers and generated complete ansatz structures from ordered configurations. \texttt{QuECOC.py} contained the shared \ac{vqc} implementation and the direct, stacked and coherent ensemble models. \texttt{Exploration.py} generated the experimental configurations, assigned deterministic identifiers and executed the repeated training runs.

Structured outputs from the execution framework were consumed by a Streamlit analysis dashboard. The storage and reproducibility procedure is described in Section~\ref{sec:method-reproducibility-scope}, while the available rankings and comparisons are defined in Section~\ref{sec:method-evaluation-ranking}.

\subsection{Dataset Selection}

14 multiclass classification datasets were chosen from the \ac{uci} Machine Learning Repository. The datasets were selected to provide variation in sample count, feature dimensionality, class count, class balance and feature type. This allowed the models to be evaluated across a range of classification conditions rather than a collection of structurally similar problems.

Eight main datasets were used for the individual \ac{vqc} architecture search: Iris, Balance Scale, Contraceptive Method Choice, Heart Disease, Obesity, Image Segmentation, Steel Plates and Yeast. This group included small and large datasets, numerical and categorical features, low and high class counts, and different levels of \gls{class-imbalance}. The main datasets were used to identify architecture characteristics that performed consistently across different problems before the candidate set was reduced for the ensemble experiments.

Six stress datasets were introduced after the \ac{vqc} architecture shortlisting: Waveform, \ac{ctg} 10 Classes, Wine Quality, Nursery, Optical Recognition and Letter Recognition. These datasets extended the evaluation through larger sample counts, greater feature dimensionality, higher class counts and more complex class distributions. In particular, Optical Recognition and Letter Recognition increased the number of target classes, while Nursery introduced a larger categorical dataset and Wine Quality provided a strongly imbalanced ordinal classification problem.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Data Splits}

Each dataset was manually inspected before modelling to identify the target variable, feature types, missing-value markers and dataset-specific formatting requirements. Numerical, ordinal and categorical variables were distinguished according to the accompanying \ac{uci} documentation. Invalid values and duplicated headers were removed where necessary, while categorical variables converted into numerical mappings using One Hot Encoding. 

A single \glslink{stratified-split}{stratified 80:20 train--test split} was created for each dataset using a fixed random state, and preprocessing operations requiring fitted values, including imputation and scaling, were fitted using the training partition only. The fully processed datasets were saved and used across all the models and stages, ensuring that performance differences resulted from the model configurations rather than changes in the sampled data.

Validation and cross-validation partitions were derived exclusively from the training set. The test partition was not used for preprocessing, early stopping, learner weighting, meta-model training or candidate selection. It was accessed only after training to provide the final evaluation of each completed model.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{sec:method-training}

All trainable quantum models used mini-batch optimisation with the Adam optimiser and negative log-likelihood loss. Training was limited to a maximum of 200 epochs and used a validation subset derived from the training partition. The training was stopped early when the validation loss failed to improve for the defined patience interval, following an initial warm-up period. The model state associated with the lowest validation loss was restored before test evaluation. The learning rate, batch size and early-stopping settings were held constant within each experimental stage.

Each quantum configuration was trained multiple times to obtain a distribution of performance results. This provided a measure of the variation introduced by quantum-circuit initialisation and optimisation. The deterministic classical reference models were evaluated once for each dataset because repeating them with an unchanged configuration would produce the same result.

\section{Stage I: Classical Reference Baselines}

This stage was implemented using the \texttt{SVC} and \texttt{RandomForestClassifier} estimator classes. Their shared estimator interface provided a consistent training and prediction workflow while retaining the distinct kernel-based and tree-based behaviour of the two model families.

\subsection{Models}

The two model families provide distinct classical reference points. Their configurations remained fixed across all datasets so that the comparison reflected differences between model families rather than dataset-specific hyperparameter optimisation.

The resulting scores established the reference performance available before circuit architecture and quantum ensemble variables were introduced. They were used as practical points of comparison for the later models rather than as capacity-matched claims about the relative computational power of classical and quantum learning.

\section{Stage II: \acs*{vqc} Component Analysis}
\label{sec:method-vqc-search}

The custom \texttt{VQC} class provided the common implementation for every individual quantum classifier tested during this stage. This search formed the component-level part of the dissertation's main analysis, identifying how the information and trainable capacity available to the learners could affect the framework built from them. The class resolved the selected features and ansatz template, constructed the PennyLane QNode and PyTorch quantum layer, and converted measured basis-state probabilities into normalised class probabilities. A consistent \texttt{fit}, \texttt{predict\_proba}, \texttt{predict} and \texttt{score} interface was retained across the architecture search, together with functions for resetting a model and exporting its configuration, training history and results. Optimisation and model restoration followed the shared procedure defined in Section~\ref{sec:method-training}.

\subsection{Parametric \acs*{vqc} and Ansatz Representation}

The individual \ac{vqc} formed the initial quantum baseline and supplied the circuit structures used by the later ensemble models. Each candidate was defined by its qubit count, selected input features and ordered ansatz specification.

A custom class was built to represent each ansatz as an ordered sequence of layer names and their associated parameters. Layers could be dynamically added and individually configured, allowing for various different ansatz designs to be easily implemented  without the need to implement a separate circuit class for every configuration. \ac{vqc}-level properties, including feature selection, were stored separately from the circuit-layer definitions. It also calculated the number of trainable parameters and encoded features, checked feature coverage and validated that each layer consumed the expected number of parameters. Template configurations were designed, allowing common structures to be initialised consistently and traced throughout the experimental process.

The circuit output was obtained using minimum measurement, with $\lceil\log_2(C)\rceil$ wires measured for a problem containing $C$ classes. The resulting computational-basis probabilities were assigned to the target classes using a fixed class-state mapping and normalised before calculating the loss or generating predictions. Measurement width remained fixed according to the class count and was not included as an architecture-search variable.

\subsection{Architecture Variables}

The architecture search was designed around characteristics identified in previous \ac{vqc} research as influential to circuit performance and trainability. These included circuit width, depth, the relationship between width and depth, \gls{encoding-capacity}, feature reuploading, trainable-parameter distribution and entangling structure. Increasing circuit size can expand representational capacity, but can also increase resource requirements and optimisation difficulty. These effects depend on both the number of qubits and the number and arrangement of circuit layers~\cite{incudini_resource_2023,cerezo_challenges_2022,du_quantum_2025}.

Circuit width was controlled through the number of qubits, while depth was varied through the number of encoding, \glspl{trainable-layer} and \glspl{entangling-layer}. Crossing these variables allowed the search to compare different depth-to-width relationships, including narrower circuits with repeated processing and wider circuits with fewer operations per qubit. Entangling gates were applied between successive reupload blocks, while the \texttt{trainable\_layers} sequence specified how many trainable \ac{rx} and \ac{rz} layers occurred before each entangling position. Uniform, increasing, decreasing, front-loaded and back-loaded arrangements distinguished circuits with similar overall sizes but different distributions of trainable parameters, since layer placement can affect both resource use and the contribution of individual operations~\cite{salmenpera_impact_2024}.

The amount of available input information requested by a circuit was controlled through \gls{feature-density}. A density of 1.0 requested the complete available feature set, while lower fractional values deliberately restricted the circuit to a proportion of that information. The separate \texttt{feats\_per\_qubit} parameter controlled the \gls{features-per-qubit} during every reupload. Input data were encoded using either single-feature angle rotations or pairwise encoding. Angle encoding applied input-dependent \acs{rx} and \acs{rz} rotations, while pairwise encoding used \ac{rzz} rotations parameterised by the product of two features. The number of data reuploads was also varied, allowing additional features or repeated information to be introduced without increasing circuit width. Features were distributed between reuploads using block or cyclic allocation~\cite{du_quantum_2025,mcfarthing_classical_2024}.

Entangling capability was varied through both the two-qubit gate and its interaction pattern. Fixed \ac{cz} gates and trainable \acs{rzz} rotations were evaluated using linear and parallel patterns. The linear pattern applied adjacent interactions sequentially, while the parallel pattern grouped non-overlapping adjacent interactions within the same entangling stage. This allowed the effect of entangling placement to be compared while retaining the same available two-qubit operations. For two-qubit circuits, both patterns produced the same connection, so duplicate operations were removed. All candidate architectures were adapted to the Heron-oriented gate family used by the project, comprising \acs{rx}, \acs{rz}, \acs{cz} and \acs{rzz} operations.

\subsection{Search Execution and Evaluation}

The individual-circuit search evaluated each \ac{vqc} as a complete multiclass classifier. This differs from the subsequent ensemble stages, in which each base \ac{vqc} learns one binary class split produced by the \ac{ecoc} decomposition. The individual search therefore identified architectures capable of learning the more complex multiclass patterns without assuming that their relative ordering would necessarily be preserved when they were retrained on binary ensemble tasks.

Every valid \ac{vqc} configuration was evaluated on the eight primary datasets using the common preprocessing procedure and fixed stratified train--test splits. The complete search contained 89,856 configurations per dataset, producing 718,848 configuration--dataset results. Each configuration was trained five times, with the data and architecture held constant while the parameter-initialisation seed varied between runs, resulting in a total of 3,594,240 fitted models.

For every run, parameter count, qubit count and training time were recorded alongside the predictive results. This allowed the subsequent ranking to distinguish architectures with similar classification performance but different computational requirements.

Configurations were ranked separately within each dataset using their mean test \acl{f1} across the five initialisations. These ranks were converted to percentiles and then averaged across the eight datasets, so that a higher average percentile indicated stronger and more consistent relative performance across datasets of different difficulty.

The primary performance analysis was restricted to the configurations for which feature density was 1.0. This full-density setting requested the total available feature set and could therefore be used to assess the full predictive strength of each architecture, subject to the circuit's attainable encoding capacity.

The remaining density settings were retained for a separate robustness analysis. Because these settings provided each circuit with only part of the available feature information, they were not treated as directly comparable measures of maximum predictive performance. Instead, they were used to identify architectures whose relative performance remained stable as the amount of available input information was reduced.

The search evaluated widths of two, four, six and eight qubits and did not extend beyond eight-qubit circuits. It therefore examined ansatz behaviour within a relatively small-circuit regime rather than the optimal performance attainable from quantum models.

\subsection{\acs*{vqc} Ranking and Shortlisting}

The cross-dataset ranking was examined alongside grouped architecture comparisons to identify characteristics associated with performance, stability and training cost. This included comparisons based on qubit count, feature density, encoding, reuploads, trainable-layer arrangement and entangling structure.

Several strong configurations were retained for the ensemble stage rather than selecting a single highest-ranked circuit. The shortlist preserved variation in architecture and included resource-efficient configurations that remained close to the leading predictive results. Consistently uncompetitive designs were excluded because carrying the full architecture search into the more expensive ensemble experiments would provide limited additional evidence.

\section{Stage III: \texttt{QuantumECOC} Ensemble Analysis}
\label{sec:method-direct-ensemble}

The \texttt{QuantumECOC} class was the central multiclass implementation evaluated in this stage. Given an ensemble configuration, it generated a deterministic \ac{ecoc} matrix, constructed the required \texttt{VQC} base learners and retained the mappings connecting target classes, code columns, circuit structures and learner-specific features. It also handled input scaling, decomposition of the multiclass labels into binary targets and deterministic stratified sampling for the individual learner training sets. Once trained, the class combined learner outputs through validation-weighted \ac{ecoc} support, produced multiclass probabilities and predictions, and recorded the ensemble structure, learner-level results, training history and resource requirements.

The architectures retained from Stage II supplied the reusable base-component pool. Together, Stages II and III formed the main analysis of the dissertation. This stage moved from individual component behaviour to the complete \texttt{QuantumECOC} design space, examining how the information and decision capacity available to the system changed through learner count, component composition and feature allocation while the underlying multiclass decomposition and training interface remained consistent.

\subsection{\acs*{ecoc} Decomposition and Base Learners}

\ac{ecoc} were used to decompose each multiclass classification problem into a collection of binary subtasks. Each target class was assigned a binary \gls{codeword}, while each column of the code matrix defined the class partition learned by one base classifier. This reduced the complexity of the task assigned to each circuit and made the design and information capacity of the small base \acp{vqc} the principal learner-level variables. The different class partitions also created diversity between the learners because each column represented a distinct binary view of the original problem. The code matrices were generated deterministically so that matched ensemble configurations used the same binary decomposition.

Each binary subtask was learned using a two-qubit \ac{vqc} constructed from the architectures shortlisted during the individual \ac{vqc} search. The restricted circuit width reduced the cost of training each learner and allowed larger ensembles to be constructed without requiring an equally wide circuit for every classification task. \ac{ecoc} was used as a practical multiclass decomposition method rather than as a novel contribution to coding theory~\cite{nguyen_optimal_2021}.

\subsection{Ensemble Variability}

The ensemble search varied the number of base learners, their architecture composition and their feature allocation. Ensembles containing 6, 10 and 14 learners were evaluated, and the architecture composition was divided into homogeneous, structurally related and broadly diverse groups. Homogeneous ensembles used the same ansatz template across all learners, while the other groups combined increasing levels of variation from the shortlisted \ac{vqc} architectures. This allowed architectural diversity to be examined separately from the predictive strength of the individual candidates.

Feature subsets were varied across the base learners through \gls{feature-subspacing} even when their circuit architectures were identical, providing a method of introducing diversity while reducing the amount of information processed by each circuit~\cite{radojicic_reducing_2026}. The search included 3 feature density options of 0.25 and 0.75 and a mixed configuration, and 3 features per qubit options of 3, 5 and a mixed configuration. The block and cyclic allocation strategies from Stage II were reused at learner level, while angle, pairwise, \acs{cz} and \acs{rzz} operations were inherited from the shortlisted architectures. At two qubits, the linear and ring patterns produce the same unique connection, so only one interaction structure was used.

Some mid-performing parameter configurations were also retained because Pinto et al.~\cite{pinto_composing_2026} found that differences between learner errors can have a greater influence on ensemble performance than the individual scores alone.

\subsection{Direct Aggregation and Evaluation}

The final prediction was obtained using validation-weighted \ac{ecoc} decoding. Each learner's output was scaled by its validation performance to produce class-support values, and the class receiving the greatest combined support was selected. Only validation information derived from the training partition contributed to these weights.

Beyond the shared predictive and stability measures, ensemble results were interpreted alongside average learner quality and realised prediction diversity. This distinguished improvements associated with stronger components from those arising through complementary errors or increased learner count.

\section{Stage IV: Measured Aggregation Comparisons}
\label{sec:method-stacking}

The \texttt{StackedECOC} class provided two supporting aggregation comparisons for \texttt{QuantumECOC}. It exposed the base probability vectors required by an interchangeable classical or quantum meta-learner and supported both single-split and $k$-fold meta-training through a common prediction and result-recording interface. This stage tested whether a learned interpretation of the same measured learner outputs was more effective than validation-weighted \ac{ecoc} decoding and established matched references for the coherent innovation.

\subsection{Candidate Pool and Matched Comparison}

The stacking models used the leading configurations evaluated during the direct ensemble stage. For each matched comparison, the \ac{ecoc} matrix, \ac{vqc} architectures, feature subsets and learner count remained fixed. The direct condition retained validation-weighted decoding, while the two stackers learned from the measured base probabilities; the combiner was therefore the only altered component.

\subsection{Meta-Training Procedures}

Both procedures separated the data used to train the meta-model from the samples encountered by the corresponding base ensemble. This prevented the combiner from being trained on unrealistically favourable in-sample base predictions.

\paragraph{Single-split training.} The training partition was divided using a stratified 70:30 split. The complete base ensemble was trained using the 70\% subset, after which its predictions for the unseen 30\% subset were used as the meta-training representation. The measured probability vector produced by each binary \ac{vqc} formed the meta-features, while the original multiclass labels supplied the meta-targets. This procedure required only one base-ensemble fit but provided the meta-learner with a smaller training set and left 30\% of the available data outside the base training process.

\paragraph{$k$-fold training.} Following the \acl{oof} stacking procedure used by Demir et al.~\cite{demir_modified_2018}, the training partition was divided into $k$ stratified folds. For each fold, a complete copy of the base ensemble was trained using the remaining $k-1$ folds and evaluated on the held-out fold. The held-out probability vectors were combined until every training sample had been processed by learners that had not encountered it during training. The reported fold-based procedure used $k=3$ to balance meta-training coverage with the cost of repeatedly fitting the quantum ensemble.

The resulting \ac{oof} feature matrix was paired with the original multiclass labels and used to train the selected classical or quantum meta-learner. Preprocessing and feature scaling were fitted independently within each fold using only the fold-specific base-training data. Once meta-training was complete, the base ensemble was retrained using the full training partition. Its probability outputs were then used to construct the test meta-features supplied to the trained combiner.

\subsection{Classical Meta-Learner}

Following the use of classical meta-learning to combine measured quantum classifiers by McFarthing et al.~\cite{mcfarthing_classical_2024}, the classical stacking condition supplied the base probability vectors to an \acs{rbf}-kernel \ac{svc}. The model used $C=1.0$ and \texttt{gamma="scale"}; probability estimation was enabled and \texttt{random\_state=2} was set for reproducibility.

The \ac{svc} learned the final multiclass decision directly from the measured base-learner probabilities rather than reconstructing an \ac{ecoc} codeword through a fixed decoding rule. Its non-linear kernel allowed relationships between learner confidence values to influence the final prediction, including interactions that could not be represented through independent validation weights.

\subsection{Quantum Meta-Learner}

The quantum stacking model replaced the \ac{svc} with a fixed 12-qubit \ac{vqc}. The measured base-learner probabilities were treated as classical meta-features and re-encoded into this circuit.

The meta-learner template used a dense feature strategy within the ansatz. It contained three data reuploads using angle encoding and cyclic feature allocation. The successive reuploads contained one, two and three trainable layers respectively. \acs{cz} entanglement was enabled across all reuploads and trainable layers using a parallel entangling pattern.

The meta-\ac{vqc} was trained using the same optimisation and early-stopping procedure applied to the other trainable quantum models. Its final measured probabilities were mapped to the original multiclass labels, allowing it to learn interactions between the base outputs through a further variational quantum circuit.

\subsection{Evaluation}

The analysis examined whether learned aggregation improved upon validation-weighted \ac{ecoc} decoding, whether the quantum meta-learner provided any benefit over the classical \ac{svc} and whether either effect depended on the retained ensemble characteristics.

Training time was reported for the selected meta-training procedure. Single-split training included one base-ensemble fit and the corresponding meta-model training. The three-fold procedure included three fold-specific base-ensemble fits, construction of the complete \ac{oof} representation and final base retraining on the full training partition. Classical stacking additionally included \ac{svc} fitting, while quantum stacking included optimisation of the 12-qubit meta-\ac{vqc}. Repeated-run variation in classical stacking resulted primarily from the quantum base learners, while quantum stacking also included variation introduced by the initialisation and optimisation of the quantum meta-learner.

\section{Stage V: \texttt{CoherentECOC} Innovation}
\label{sec:method-coherent}

\texttt{CoherentECOC} was the principal methodological innovation built on top of \texttt{QuantumECOC}. It extended the direct ensemble while removing the intermediate measurement between the base learners and the meta-model. Each trained base \ac{vqc} was assigned a disjoint \gls{qubit-register}, its learned parameters were transferred into a combined circuit and a configurable meta ansatz was applied before the only final measurement. The implementation supported both \gls{main-wire}, using one representative wire from each learner, and \gls{full-register}, using every base qubit. It could rebuild the combined circuit while preserving learned weights and optimiser state. The model also supported unfreezing the complete circuit for final \gls{end-to-end-finetuning}. The base and meta resource structures were recorded alongside the final results.

\subsection{Circuit Construction}
The coherent condition begins from a selected and trained \texttt{QuantumECOC} configuration. Each base learner retains its circuit design but its terminal measurement is removed. The resulting unitary base circuits are assigned to disjoint registers within one larger QNode. The input sample is processed by every learner using the same feature mappings applied during its independent training, producing a joint state containing all base representations in quantum superposition.

A trainable meta-layer is appended after the base-circuit block and the complete circuit is measured only at its final output. The meta-circuit contains trainable and entangling operations but does not re-encode the original features or the measured probabilities used by \texttt{StackedECOC}. It therefore operates on the states prepared by the pretrained components rather than on a newly constructed classical meta-feature vector.

This construction differs from measured quantum stacking at a precise boundary. Measured stacking evaluates each base QNode, forms a classical probability vector and encodes that vector into a separate meta-\ac{vqc}. \texttt{CoherentECOC} instead composes the unitary base ansätze and the meta ansatz within one differentiable circuit. The intended mechanism is the availability of cross-register operations before measurement, although the larger joint width also increases simulation and optimisation cost~\cite{resch_quantum_2019,cerezo_challenges_2022}.

\subsection{Meta-Circuit Variability}
Two main parameters controlled how the meta-circuit interacted with the base registers. The main-wire design selected one representative qubit from every learner and applied the cross-learner operations to those wires. Its active meta width therefore grew with the number of learners. The full-register design allowed the meta-circuit to act across every base qubit, exposing more of each learner state while producing a wider and more expensive interaction space. Due to limited time and computational resources the main-wire construction was used as the principal condition.

% Within these variables, the coherent search varied the number of trainable meta layers and the composition of the trainable and entangling operations. Trainable \acs{rx} and \acs{rz} rotations were combined with fixed \acs{cz} or trainable \acs{rzz} interactions, while interaction order and distance controlled which learner registers could exchange information. These variables changed the amount and structure of cross-learner processing available after the base circuits rather than changing the information originally assigned to each learner.

% Minimum final measurement was used as the principal readout so that only the number of wires required to represent the target classes was measured. Where included as a restricted comparison, a wider readout exposed additional basis states. In both cases, computational-basis outcomes were assigned through a fixed class-state mapping and the resulting class probabilities were normalised before loss calculation or prediction.

\subsection{Coherent Training Procedures}

\texttt{CoherentECOC} supported the same single-split and $k$-fold separation used by the measured stacking class. In both cases, the base circuits were trained before the coherent meta-layer so that its parameters were learned using samples that were unseen by the corresponding base learners.

For $k$-fold training, the learned ansätze and parameters were transferred into the unmeasured circuit, and the coherent meta parameters were updated using the held-out fold. One shared meta-parameter state and optimiser state were preserved across folds, while the fold-specific base learners were replaced before the next iteration. The reported fold-based procedure used $k=3$. After all held-out folds had contributed to meta-training, the base ensemble was retrained using the complete training set. The coherent circuit was rebuilt using these final base ansätze and parameters while retaining the learned meta parameters.

Once the intitial training was complete, the full circuit could be unfrozen and fine-tuned end to end using the complete training partition, allowing the pretrained base representations and coherent aggregation layer to adapt jointly~\cite{friedrich_quantum_2025,du_quantum_2025}.

\subsection{Coherent Evaluation and Finalist Selection}
Coherent variants were constructed only for a reduced set of strong direct and measured-ensemble candidates as the complete circuit width was the sum of the retained base-register widths. For every matched comparison, the dataset split, \ac{ecoc} matrix, learner count, base architectures, feature assignments and independently trained base tasks were retained. The newly introduced variables were therefore the coherent ansatz pattern, meta-circuit structure, final measurement width and whether final end-to-end fine-tuning was applied.

The coherent results were compared with validation-weighted direct decoding, classical stacking and measured quantum stacking built from the corresponding ensemble definition. The evaluation used mean macro-\acs{f1}, repeated-run variation, convergence and total training time, together with the complete circuit width, depth and trainable-parameter count. These measures were used to determine whether the additional information available before measurement produced a predictive or stability benefit sufficient to justify the joint circuit and its increased optimisation requirements. Cross-register gates provide the interaction being tested, but also create the principal resource cost of the coherent extension~\cite{resch_quantum_2019,park_addressing_2025}.

\section{Cross-Stage Evaluation and Ranking}
\label{sec:method-evaluation-ranking}

A common evaluation framework was used across the classical references, individual \acp{vqc}, direct quantum ensembles and the three aggregation extensions. The accumulated results were analysed through the interactive dashboard, which dynamically generated overall and dataset-specific rankings, configurable comparisons and repeated-run distributions. Results could be filtered by model family, circuit architecture, ensemble composition, aggregation method, meta-learner and resource requirements.

\subsection{Common Predictive and Stability Metrics}

Following the imbalance-sensitive evaluation used in previous classification studies~\cite{demir_modified_2018,vasan_exploring_2025}, \acl{f1} was treated as the primary predictive metric. This assigned equal importance to every class and prevented performance on majority classes from dominating the evaluation, accuracy and weighted-\acs{f1} were reported as secondary aggregate measures.

Each trainable quantum configuration was evaluated across repeated runs. Following the use of repeated observations to evaluate both performance and stability in \ac{vqc} ensembles~\cite{radojicic_reducing_2026}, the mean, minimum, maximum and standard deviation were calculated for each recorded metric. The individual run results were retained so that distributions and variation between initialisation seeds could be examined rather than reporting only the mean result.

Applying the same measures throughout the experimental progression allowed changes in performance to be followed as circuit architectures, ensemble compositions and aggregation methods were introduced.

\subsection{Global Ranking}

Each configuration was ranked by its mean \acl{f1} within each dataset, with equal scores receiving the same rank. The resulting per-dataset ranks were then averaged across all evaluated datasets to produce the global ranking. Every dataset contributed equally regardless of its sample count, feature dimensionality or number of classes, preventing larger datasets from exerting greater influence over the final ordering.

The dashboard dynamically generated these rankings for the selected model families and experimental stages. Rankings could also be grouped by aggregation method, learner count, architecture composition, circuit variables and resource characteristics. This allowed the strongest complete configurations to be identified while also examining whether particular design choices performed consistently across multiple datasets.

Average rank was used as the principal cross-dataset comparison because absolute \acl{f1} values were not directly comparable between datasets with different levels of difficulty. However, the ranking was interpreted alongside the original \acl{f1} values and repeated-run variation. This prevented small differences in rank from being treated as meaningful when the underlying predictive results were similar or unstable.

\subsection{Progressive Selection Criteria}
\label{sec:method-progressive-selection}

Following the progressive model-selection approach used by Demir et al.~\cite{demir_modified_2018}, fewer candidates were advanced as the cost of the experiments increased. Advancement decisions used the cross-dataset ranking defined above, but were not determined by rank alone. Raw \acl{f1}, repeated-run stability and resource requirements were examined alongside the ordering, and consistently uncompetitive configurations were excluded from the more expensive stages.

The retained pool included several circuit structures and feature densities rather than a single winner. This maintained coverage of distinct design characteristics and prevented later conclusions from depending on one architecture family. Lower-cost configurations were also advanced when their predictive results remained competitive.

Two forms of analysis were then applied. Controlled comparisons used identical base configurations to isolate the effect of validation-weighted, classical-stacked and measured-quantum aggregation. The stacked comparisons retained the same ensemble definition for a smaller feasible subset. Grouped analysis used the wider candidate pool to examine relationships involving learner count, architecture composition and feature allocation.

\section{Reproducibility and Computational Scope}
\label{sec:method-reproducibility-scope}

Fixed stratified data splits, preprocessing values, feature mappings and label mappings were retained for each dataset. Feature allocation, \ac{ecoc} generation, fold construction and learner sampling used deterministic seeds, while the parameter-initialisation seed was recorded separately and varied between repeated quantum runs.

Each completed run was stored in \ac{jsonl} format using a deterministic configuration identifier. The stored information included the model configuration, selected features, ansatz structure, training history, timing information and evaluation results. The search spaces, ensemble assignments and shortlisted configurations were also retained, allowing the dashboard rankings and comparisons to be regenerated without repeating the experiments.

The experimental scope was bounded by the increasing cost of statevector simulation and repeated ensemble training. Circuit width increased the simulated state space. Single-split stacking required one base-ensemble fit before meta-training, whereas the three-fold procedure required three fold-specific fits followed by final base retraining on the complete training partition. These costs determined the candidate budgets used at the later stages and are treated as boundaries of the experimental design rather than as failed experiments.

The dense group produced the highest median at ten learners, while the uniform group also performed strongly at fourteen. In contrast, combining all retained layouts did not consistently outperform the more restricted groups. Architectural variation therefore did not provide an automatic benefit, an ensemble of consistently strong layouts could outperform a broader mixture that also introduced weaker base designs.

The distributions nevertheless overlap, showing that learner count and layout group do not fully determine the result. Their interaction is more informative than either factor alone. Increasing the number of learners expanded the number of binary class partitions represented in the final decision, but the benefit remained dependent on whether those learners used sufficiently effective circuit layouts.

\subsection{Feature Allocation and Encoding Design}
\label{subsec:direct-feature-design}

Figure~\ref{fig:direct-feature-design} compares the remaining feature and encoding choices. Feature density produced the clearest separation, with the ordering of the distributions following the proportion of the original input made available to the learners. The stronger performance of the higher-density configurations therefore appears to reflect their broader feature coverage rather than an advantage associated with the parameter value itself. Lower-density ensembles introduced greater variation between learners, but did so by restricting the information available for each binary classification problem. The mixed configurations occupied an intermediate position, with top configurations achieving significantly higher performance, suggesting that this trade-off could be beneficial.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/plot7.png}
    \caption{Distribution of average cross-dataset rank percentile by feature density, encoding-gate composition and features per qubit.}
    \label{fig:direct-feature-design}
\end{figure}

The difference between the features-per-qubit groups was smaller, with substantial overlap once enough encoding positions were available. This again indicates that increasing a single parameter is not independently responsible for stronger performance. The more important distinction was whether the combined configuration provided sufficient space to represent the available information without excessively restricting each learner.

\subsection*{Stage III Summary}

Overall, the direct ensemble search shows that diversity was most effective after the information requirements of the base learners had been satisfied. Increasing learner count, varying feature assignments and combining circuit layouts could produce complementary predictions, but these benefits weakened when the individual circuits were given insufficient feature coverage or encoding capacity. Strong \texttt{QuantumECOC} configurations therefore balanced variation between learners with the need to preserve informative representations within each learner. These insights provide the fixed base configurations used in the subsequent comparisons of learned aggregation methods.

These relationships describe the evaluated two-qubit binary \ac{ecoc} learners. Increasing the width of the base circuits or replacing the binary decomposition with multiclass learners may change the balance between individual learner capacity, task diversity and the number of components required by the ensemble.
